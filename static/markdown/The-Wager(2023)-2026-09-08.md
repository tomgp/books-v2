Ususally, even if I don't really connect with a book, I can understand where praise is coming from or why it's successful or whatever, but The Wager, David Grann's "[New masterpiece](https://www.theguardian.com/books/2023/apr/29/the-wager-review-david-grann-shipwreck-epic)", I don't get it. It's fine, decently written and an interesting story. But I'm not sure I got much more from reading it than I would have from [wikipedia's summary of the incidents it relates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wager_Mutiny). (espescially as said summary has maps, a feature that's bafflingly missing from the book)

I think I have a fundamental distrust of narative non-fiction. Sure there are copious end-notes and sources and so on, but when the authour tells us how someone who lived hundreds of years ago is feeling as they trek across a swamp or look out over a view I can't help but just think "bullshit". 

Most of the time I found myself just wanting more detail; an interesting discussion of scurvy through the ages, some discussion of the trends in naval battle tactics at the time etc. but the "plot" demands we move on. I think there's something deeper to my reservations though; I think imposing a neatly structured plot, a begining, a middle and an end, on a slice of history just feels at odds with how I experience the world to be. I find not just the detail (see above) but the whole structure dishonest, at odds with the idea of non-fiction. 

Deep down I'm just not a narrative person. I don't mean that I don't enjoy narrative in fiction -- I do! (though i've definately come to realise that my interests, broadly speaking, don't lie in that area.) What I mean is that I don't experience my life or the world in general as a story * and so when people present history or factual stuff in to storyish a fashion it doesn't sit right with me.

---

\* cf Galen Strawson's [Against Narrativiy](https://lchc.ucsd.edu/mca/Paper/against_narrativity.pdf), never have I felt so 'seen' by an academic paper.

Recently we've found out a lot about how different brains experience the world. Bumping into my old school friend Dave at a party, either just before or just after the pandemic, he told me how he'd been diagnosed as 'aphantasic'. I'd never heard of the condition at the time but the diea really hit me hard. Whilst we've broadend our understanding and acceptance of differences in people's brains, esp. through the tireless and thankless work of autistic people, my feeling is that the range of ways in which we percieve, process and organise the world is stil far broader than we typically imagine. The gulf between the narrativists account of their experience of then world and how I go through life is so vast it feels like a similar thing to aphantasia and suggests to me all sorts of other unsuspected axes along which we might vary in intersting ways.
